THE DIVINE

The Philippine stories and the Anitism religion overlap, they are interconnected. Scriptures are a collection of stories that explain the origins of the world, natural phenomena, and the actions of Gods, Goddesses, spirits, and heroes. It serves as a cultural narrative, often tied to the beliefs of a community. Anitism, on the other hand, encompasseses the spiritual practices, rituals, and theology rooted in those beliefs. Scripture is a component of religion, while religion is a broader system that includes worship and rituals. The stories are deeply tied to the Anitism religion, and its pantheon of deities, ancestor spirits, and nature spirits. For example, "Diwata" refers to Gods and Goddesses.

While scripture focused on narratives about these beings, Anitism involved rituals to honor or appease them. Over time, stories evolved into legends or folktales, especially as they became detached from their religious roots. Many aspects of Philippine stories survived and became scriptures within the Anitism religion. The stories of deities, spirits, and heroes remain integral to the Philippine culture, showcasing the distinction between the historical narratives and the spiritual practices tied to them.

All that we have been able to learn has been handed down from family members from the older generation to the newer generation in tradition, and is preserved in their customs; and in some songs that they retain in their memory and repeat when they go on the sea, sung to the time of their rowing, and in their merrymakings, feasts, and funerals, and even in their work, when many of them work together. In those songs are recounted the fabulous genealogies and vain deeds of their Gods and Goddesses. Among their Gods and Goddesses is one who is the chief and superior to all the others. The Visayas call the Goddess Laon, which denotes the “antiquity” or “ancient.”

They adored animals and birds; and the sun and moon. They also attributed to the rainbow its kind of divinity. There was no old tree to which they did not attribute divine honors, and it was a sacrilege to think of cutting it under any consideration. Even the very rocks, crags, reefs, and points along the seashore and rivers were adored, and an offering made to them on passing, by stopping there and placing the offering upon the rock or reef. When sailing to the island of Panay, one saw on the point called Nasso, near Potol, a rock upon which were dishes and other pieces of crockery-ware, which were offered to it by those who went on the sea.

They also adored private taotao (ancestor spirits and possibly other spirits represent or house them), which each one inherited from his ancestors. The Visayans called them Diwata. In memory of their ancestors they kept certain very small and made taotao of stone, wood, gold, or ivory, called licha or laravan. Among their Gods they reckoned also all those who perished by the sword, or who were devoured by crocodiles, as well as those killed by lightning. They thought that the souls of such immediately ascended to the blest abode by means of the rainbow, called by them Balangaw. Generally, whoever could succeed in it attributed divinity to his aged father at his death.

DIVINE ENTITIES AND FIGURES (ILONGGO)

IMMORTALS:

Laon: the supreme Goddess and creator residing in Mount Kanlaon; governs the harvest, pestilence, and locusts.

Makaptan: God of the earth.

Magwayen: Goddess of the sea.

Sidapa: God who lives in the sacred Mount Madia-as; determines the day of a person's death by marking every newborn's lifespan on a very tall tree on Madya-as.

Pandaki: God who is given ritual offerings so that a soul of the deceased will not be taken by the Gods responsible for torment in the afterlife.

Gods of torment:

  • Simuran: tormentor God.

  • Siginarugan: tormentor God.

Gods of war:

  • Balangaw: the rainbow.

  • Inaginid: war God.

  • Makanduk: war God.
